Frequently asked questions

What is billing demand and how does an ESS lower it?

For industrial HV customers the demand charge is not the contract demand but the billing demand multiplied by the rate (option II 8,320 KRW/kW). Billing demand is the highest 15 minute peak among December, January, February, July, August, September and the current month within the last 12 months, and it cannot be lower than 30% of the contract demand (Article 68 of the KEPCO basic supply terms). One summer or winter peak sets the demand charge for a whole year, so when an ESS discharges at that moment and keeps grid supply under the target, the demand charge falls for all twelve months.

Which is larger, the demand charge saving or the energy arbitrage?

It depends on the factory. A factory with short, sharp peaks cuts many kW with a small battery, so the demand charge saving dominates. A factory with long, flat peaks can cut few kW, so charging at night off peak (option II summer 116.0 KRW) and discharging on peak (229.0 KRW) earns the larger share. The savings breakdown table above shows both. The charging energy cost and the efficiency loss are already subtracted.

Which option, I, II or III, is better?

By KEPCO guidance option I suits customers with 200 or fewer monthly use hours (load factor), option II 200 to 500 hours, and option III over 500 hours. Monthly use hours are the monthly kWh divided by the billing demand. An ESS lowers the billing demand and so raises the use hours, so the option should be reviewed together. Change ① and compare the three options.

Why is the payback a range?

The EMS cannot fully know tomorrow's load, so it sets the target a little high. The calculator runs both the realistic case with the margin in ⑬ and the ideal case with a margin of 0 and shows the range between them. The payback year is then counted on a 20 year cash flow with the decline in ⑪ and the operating cost in ⑨. In a real study the same calculation on a full year of 15 minute KEPCO data narrows the range.

How do I choose the ESS size (kW and kWh)?

The PCS kW sets how much of the peak can be cut, and the battery kWh sets how long that cut can be held and how much can be discharged in the daytime. For sharp peaks use less kWh per kW; for daytime arbitrage give it more kWh. The size comparison table pairs half, one and one and a half times the kW in ⑥ with 2, 3 and 4 hour batteries and shows yearly savings and payback side by side, so get quotes starting from the combination with the shortest payback.

What changes with a study on real 15 minute data?

This calculator builds the same load shape every day from monthly use and peak demand. A real factory's peak moves with weather, production plans and holidays, which changes how many kW can be cut and how much room is left for charging. Download a year of 15 minute data from KEPCO Power Planner and send it to us, and we run the same rules on the measured data. The first review is free.
