Factory ESS peak shaving payback calculator (Korea)

Demand charge savings, night charging arbitrage and a 20 year payback range for a peak shaving ESS on the KEPCO industrial (B) tariff.

What the ESS peak shaving calculator tells you

For industrial HV customers in Korea the demand charge is the billing demand multiplied by the rate, not the contract demand. Billing demand is the highest 15 minute peak among December, January, February, July, August, September and the current month within the last 12 months, with a floor of 30% of the contract demand (Article 68 of the KEPCO basic supply terms). One peak sets the demand charge for a year, so an ESS that discharges at that moment lowers twelve months of demand charges.

The calculator builds a year of 35,040 fifteen minute load points from two measured factory profiles and runs a rule based dispatch (peak cut → night charging → arbitrage discharge) that forecasts only from the last 14 days, like a real EMS. Demand charge savings and off peak charging arbitrage are shown separately, and a 20 year cash flow with battery decline and operating cost gives the payback range.

A real study uses a full year of 15 minute KEPCO Power Planner data. The calculator models the load from monthly use and peak demand, so results differ from a measured study. The first review is free.

Frequently asked questions

What is billing demand and how does an ESS lower it?

For industrial HV customers the demand charge is not the contract demand but the billing demand multiplied by the rate (option II 8,320 KRW/kW). Billing demand is the highest 15 minute peak among December, January, February, July, August, September and the current month within the last 12 months, and it cannot be lower than 30% of the contract demand (Article 68 of the KEPCO basic supply terms). One summer or winter peak sets the demand charge for a whole year, so when an ESS discharges at that moment and keeps grid supply under the target, the demand charge falls for all twelve months.

Which is larger, the demand charge saving or the energy arbitrage?

It depends on the factory. A factory with short, sharp peaks cuts many kW with a small battery, so the demand charge saving dominates. A factory with long, flat peaks can cut few kW, so charging at night off peak (option II summer 116.0 KRW) and discharging on peak (229.0 KRW) earns the larger share. The savings breakdown table above shows both. The charging energy cost and the efficiency loss are already subtracted.

Which option, I, II or III, is better?

By KEPCO guidance option I suits customers with 200 or fewer monthly use hours (load factor), option II 200 to 500 hours, and option III over 500 hours. Monthly use hours are the monthly kWh divided by the billing demand. An ESS lowers the billing demand and so raises the use hours, so the option should be reviewed together.
