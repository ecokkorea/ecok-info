Korea industrial electricity tariff 2026: rates, demand charges and a worked bill

KEPCO industrial rates by class, voltage, option, season and time band in KRW, the billing demand rule, and a full winter bill of 27,347,780 KRW worked line by line.

Tariffs · ECO K KOREA engineering team · 2026-11-12

The short answer

As of November 2026, a Korean factory pays between 88.0 and 242.9 KRW per kWh for energy, depending on its tariff class, the season and the time of day, plus a monthly demand charge of 5,550 to 9,810 KRW per kW. On top of those come a climate charge of 9.0 KRW/kWh, a fuel cost adjustment of 5.0 KRW/kWh, 10% VAT and a 2.7% power industry fund.

Foreign owners and EPC contractors usually meet the Korean industrial tariff through a KEPCO bill they cannot read, or through a feasibility model that uses one flat rate. Both hide the two things that drive a factory bill here: a demand charge that is set by a single 15 minute peak, and energy rates that differ by almost two to one between night and afternoon. This article lays out the 2026 tariff in one place, in KRW, with a worked bill.

All rates below are taken from the KEPCO tariff table in force since 1 June 2026 (the industrial (B) class since 16 April 2026) and are quoted before VAT and the fund.

How the industrial tariff is organised

KEPCO splits industrial customers by contract demand. Industrial (A), written 산업용(갑) on the bill, covers contract demand under 300 kW. Industrial (B), 산업용(을), covers 300 kW and above and is always billed by time of day. Within each class the supply voltage sets the row: low voltage at 220 or 380 V, high voltage A from 3.3 kV to 66 kV (the 22.9 kV service that most factories take), high voltage B at 154 kV and high voltage C at 345 kV.

High voltage customers then choose an option, numbered I, II and III. Each step up raises the monthly demand charge and lowers the energy rate, so the right option depends on how many hours a month the plant runs at its billing demand. KEPCO's own tariff table describes option I as suited to customers with few hours of use, option II to customers with many, and option III to customers using more than 500 hours a month, where hours of use means monthly kWh divided by billing demand.

High voltage B customers at 154 kV pay demand charges of 6,630, 7,380 and 8,190 KRW/kW for options I, II and III, and high voltage C at 345 kV pays 6,590, 7,520 and 8,090. Their energy rates sit slightly above the high voltage A rows; the highest single rate in the table, 242.9 KRW/kWh, is the summer on peak rate for high voltage B option I. Industrial (B) customers also get a 50% discount on energy used between 11:00 and 14:00 on Saturdays, Sundays and public holidays in spring and autumn.

The time bands

  • Off peak: 22:00 to 08:00 in every season.
  • On peak: 15:00 to 21:00 in summer, spring and autumn; 09:00 to 12:00 and 16:00 to 19:00 in winter.
  • Mid peak: every other hour.
  • On Saturdays, on peak energy is billed at the mid peak rate. On Sundays and public holidays, both energy and maximum demand are recorded as off peak.

The demand charge is not applied to contract demand but to billing demand: the largest 15 minute maximum demand among December, January, February, July, August and September of the previous twelve months and the current month, with a floor of 30% of contract demand. One high quarter hour in January therefore sets the demand charge until the following January. We explain the rule and the ways to lower it in our note on lowering a factory demand charge.

That is 202.2 KRW/kWh on the electricity charge and 227.9 KRW/kWh on the amount billed. If the plant could move its 30,000 on peak kWh into the off peak band, the energy line would fall by 30,000 × (204.6 − 123.0) = 2,448,000 KRW in that month alone, which is why load shifting and storage are the first things we look at for Korean factories.

What foreign models usually get wrong

  • Using one blended rate. A Korean factory running a day shift pays a very different average from a plant running three shifts, because of the on peak and off peak split.
  • Treating the demand charge as a function of contract demand. It is set by billing demand, and billing demand is set by the worst 15 minutes of the summer and winter months.
  • Forgetting the add ons. Climate charge, fuel cost adjustment, VAT and the 2.7% fund together add roughly 25% to the headline energy rate in the example above.
  • Converting to dollars too early. KEPCO revises the energy and demand rates by notice, and the fuel cost adjustment changes every quarter (+5.0 KRW/kWh for October to December 2026), so a model should carry KRW rates with a date on them.
