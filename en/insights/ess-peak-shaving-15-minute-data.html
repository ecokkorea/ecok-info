How we size ESS for peak shaving with 15 minute data

Korean industrial tariffs bill the highest 15 minute demand for a year. Our method: a full year of KEPCO data, two dispatch models and a capacity sweep.

Energy storage · ECO K KOREA engineering team · 2026-11-11

Why 15 minute data

In Korea the monthly basic charge of an industrial customer is set by the single highest 15 minute demand, and that peak can follow the customer for a year. An ESS sized for peak shaving therefore has to be sized from the full year of 15 minute readings, not from monthly bills.

Most of our ESS work is for factories on the KEPCO industrial (B) tariff. The tariff is simple to read and easy to misjudge, so we built our own analysis engine around the 15 minute meter data that KEPCO lets every customer download. This article walks through the method and one published case.

The tariff in four facts

ItemIndustrial (B), high voltage A, option II (2026)
Basic charge8,320 KRW per kW of billing demand per month
Billing demandThe highest of this month's 15 minute peak, the highest peak of December, January, February, July, August and September in the previous 12 months, and 30% of contract demand
Energy chargeOff peak 116.0 to 123.0, mid peak 133.4 to 164.0, on peak 150.9 to 229.0 KRW/kWh by season
Time bandsOff peak 22:00 to 08:00. On peak 15:00 to 21:00 in summer, spring and autumn, 09:00 to 12:00 and 16:00 to 19:00 in winter. Saturday on peak is billed as mid peak, Sundays and holidays as off peak

The ratchet in the second row is the reason a short peak is expensive. One bad quarter hour in January sets the billing demand for the following twelve months. Climate and environment charges (9.0 KRW/kWh), the fuel cost adjustment (5.0 KRW/kWh) and the 2.7% power fund are added on top, and our engine applies all of them.

Step 1: read and clean a full year

We import the KEPCO PowerPlanner export directly. A clean year has 96 intervals per day, so the first check is simple: the interval count must equal the number of days times 96. Duplicated days and gaps are repaired and the repairs are listed in the report, because a missing winter morning can hide the very peak we are trying to cut. Billing months follow the customer's meter reading day, not the calendar month.

Step 2: two ways to run the battery

We simulate every candidate system twice. The first run is a linear program (solved with HiGHS) that knows the whole year in advance. It gives the theoretical maximum saving, the same kind of number that HOMER or REopt report. The second run is a rule based controller that behaves like a real EMS: it only sees the past 14 days, sets a peak target from them with a 20 kW margin, charges at night, absorbs surplus solar and never exports. The gap between the two runs is the price of not knowing the future. We base the investment decision on the second, realistic run.

To make sure the engine agrees with the tools banks know, we ran a plant through HOMER Grid 1.11.7 with identical assumptions. The bills with no system and with solar only matched within 0.01%, the bill with solar plus ESS within 1%, and the ESS saving differed by 2.01%, as expected, because our optimal run sees the full year.

Step 3: sweep the capacity

We never size a battery from a rule of thumb. The engine runs the same year for a range of capacities, typically 250 to 2,000 kWh, and computes the saving, the 20 year cash flow, NPV, IRR and payback for each. The result is a curve, and the best size is where NPV peaks, not where savings peak.

A published case

One factory we analysed, a manufacturer with a billing demand of about 650 kW, shows why. Its demand exceeded 600 kW for only 19 hours in a whole year, and those peaks came in the morning, before rooftop solar produces much. Solar alone left the morning peak untouched, and on weekends the plant was idle and solar power was surplus.

ResultValue
Capacity sweep250 to 2,000 kWh; best band 500 to 750 kWh; 3,000 kWh not paid back within 20 years
Selected batteryAbout 630 kWh from actual product combinations
Billing demand after PV and ESSAbout 400 kW on clear days, about 510 kW on the worst day without solar
Electricity bill indexToday 100, solar only 68, solar plus ESS 58
Simple paybackSolar only 5.7 years, solar plus ESS 6.4 years, ESS alone 8.8 years

We valued the saving at the worst day, about 510 kW, because billing demand is set by the worst quarter hour of the year, not by an average sunny day. Even on that basis, adding the battery gave about 17% more 20 year net present value than solar alone. The calculation used a 20 year period, a 4.5% discount rate and no tariff increase; any tariff increase shortens the payback.

The same method gives a different answer elsewhere. A plant that peaks at noon or runs seven days a week gets far more from solar and less from storage, and long cable routes on site can add more than a year to the ESS payback. That is why we run the sweep for every site.
