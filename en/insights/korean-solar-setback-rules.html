Korean solar permitting setback rules explained

Since September 2026 Korean local governments can only apply house setbacks of up to 200 m and a few zone setbacks to solar. How we measure, count and report them.

Permitting · ECO K KOREA engineering team · 2026-11-11

The short answer

Since 18 September 2026, a Korean local government can no longer impose its own setback distances on a ground mounted solar plant, with two exceptions: houses, capped at 200 m from the site boundary and only where at least five houses (or a higher count set by ordinance) fall inside that distance, and four kinds of protected or tourism zones. Road setbacks are no longer allowed for solar at all.

For anyone who screened Korean land before 2026, this is a large change. Many county ordinances used to require 500 m or more from houses and 100 m or more from roads, and a single ordinance line could kill a site. The rule now sits in Article 27-3 of the Renewable Energy Development, Use and Promotion Act and Article 27-3 of its Enforcement Decree, with the measuring method set by a Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment notice (No. 2026-235). It applies to development permit applications filed on or after 18 September 2026.

We review sites for owners and EPC contractors every week, and this article explains how we apply the new rule in practice, where the traps are, and what still has to be checked by hand.

What the law now allows

ItemRule from 18 September 2026
HousesSetback set by ordinance, no more than 200 m from the site boundary, applied only when the number of houses within that distance reaches the ordinance count (at least 5)
ZonesOrdinance setbacks may still apply in historic and cultural environment preservation areas, ecological and landscape conservation areas, tourist sites and complexes, and natural village districts
RoadsNo road setback for solar (only wind keeps a road setback, up to 500 m)
Exempt projectsResident participation projects (house setback only), building rooftop solar and self consumption solar

Two details decide most cases. First, the 200 m figure is a cap, not a default: the ordinance may set a shorter distance, and if it sets a longer one, only the legal cap is applied. Second, the house count is a threshold. If only four houses sit inside the distance and the ordinance count is five, there is no house setback at all.

How distance and houses are counted

  • Distance is the straight, shortest horizontal line from the project site boundary to the exterior wall of the house, not to the house parcel and not to the panels.
  • A house means a dwelling under the Housing Act, detached or multi unit. Warehouses, livestock sheds, shops and farm huts are not houses.
  • Each house building counts as one, so an apartment block is also one.
  • A house with no registered resident for the whole year before the permit application is not counted, and neither is a building recorded as demolished in the building register.
  • A house counts if any part of its exterior wall lies within the distance.

Our site review program measures these distances automatically from public building footprints and checks the main use of each building in the building register. Two things cannot be read from public data. Buildings missing from the register (unlicensed or very new) can be missed, and whether a household moved in during the last year is not public. For those we ask the village head or raise it with the permit office in the pre consultation meeting, and we say so in the report rather than guessing.

Old ordinances are still on the books

Many local ordinances have not yet been amended. Press reports in October 2026 counted 129 ordinances with setback clauses, of which 92 still required more than 200 m from houses and 124 still had road setbacks. An officer may therefore open the old text and quote 500 m. Our reports judge every site by the current Act and list the old ordinance figure only as a reference, marked as text that has not yet been revised. For applications filed after 18 September 2026, the Act prevails (addendum Article 3 of the Act), and we bring the ministry press material to the pre consultation.

One open question remains. When an ordinance says, for example, 500 m, it is not yet settled whether the clause is valid up to 200 m or cannot be applied at all. There is no government interpretation or court ruling yet, so we report the 200 m case as the conservative reading.

The setback that is easy to miss

Distance to neighbours is only half of it. Several ordinances also say how far the modules must stay from the edge of your own site or from the fence. In the 22 county ordinances we keep on file, nine carry such a clause: 3 m in five of them and 2 m in four. Some southwestern counties add a fence line of 1.5 to 2 m or a row of screening trees. These numbers do not change whether a site is allowed, but they change how many modules fit. On a long, narrow parcel a 3 m edge strip removes a noticeable share of the layout, so we read every clause of the solar section of the ordinance, including the annex tables, before we draw the array.

Other thresholds that come with the permit

CheckThresholdBasis
Disaster impact review5,000 m² (small scale), 50,000 m² (full)Natural Disaster Countermeasures Act, Enforcement Decree Annex 1
Heritage impact diagnosis30,000 m² of project areaNational Heritage Impact Diagnosis Act, Article 9

Land in agricultural promotion areas and preserved mountain land is a different matter: it is excluded from the layout altogether and shown on a separate map, because no setback calculation will make it usable.

When the house setback still applies

If a site fails on the house count, the usual way forward is a resident participation project. Such projects are exempt from the house setback (not from the zone setbacks). Under the RPS guidelines the plant must be 500 kW or larger, and residents of the township within 500 m, long term farmers of the adjacent village or their cooperatives invest in the project; the investment share then earns a higher REC weight. We include the steps and the investor eligibility rules in the report whenever a house setback blocks a site, so the owner can decide early.

How we report it

  • One line per rule: the current Act, the measured distance, the house count and the result, with the old ordinance figure shown as a reference.
  • A map with the site boundary drawn in bold red and every counted house numbered, so the permit office can check the count in a minute.
  • A list of items that public data cannot confirm, such as move in records, with who to ask.
  • The site edge setback from the ordinance applied in the layout and stated next to the capacity.
