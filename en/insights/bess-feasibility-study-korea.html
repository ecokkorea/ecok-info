BESS feasibility study in Korea: what a bankable analysis contains and how we cross check it

The inputs a Korean behind the meter battery study needs, the two dispatch runs, and how HOMER Grid, DER-VET, REopt, SAM and BLAST-Lite compared with our engine on one factory.

Energy storage · ECO K KOREA engineering team · 2026-11-12

The short answer

A bankable BESS feasibility study in Korea rests on four things: a full year of 15 minute meter data from KEPCO, the exact tariff with its twelve month demand ratchet and Korean holiday calendar, a dispatch that only knows the past, and a 20 year cash flow with degradation and inverter replacement. The international tools can check such a study but cannot replace it, because none of them model the KEPCO ratchet. On one factory, HOMER Grid, DER-VET and REopt agreed with our optimal dispatch within 0.1 to 5%, while SAM with its built in dispatch found only 20 to 45% of the saving.

Owners and lenders from abroad often ask us to run their preferred tool, HOMER, REopt or SAM, so that the result is in a format they know. We do, and we attach the original output pages. But the decision number comes from our own engine, and this note explains why, what the study contains and how the cross checks came out.

The method behind the engine, and the published factory case it was built on, is in our earlier note on sizing ESS with 15 minute data; a worked payback example with public tariffs is in the note on peak shaving payback. This note is about what makes the study credible.

Why the Korean tariff breaks foreign tools

  • The ratchet. Billing demand is the highest 15 minute demand of the winter and summer months of the previous twelve months, with a floor of 30% of contract demand (KEPCO Basic Supply Terms, Article 68). Reducing one peak lowers twelve bills. HOMER, REopt, SAM and DER-VET bill each month on its own peak, and HOMER and REopt work on hourly averages, so they see a smaller demand saving than the bill will show.
  • The calendar. Saturdays are billed at mid peak during on peak hours, Sundays and public holidays at off peak all day, and the holidays include lunar ones that move every year. DER-VET has no holiday concept and treats Saturday and Sunday alike.
  • No export. A behind the meter battery in Korea on this scheme cannot sell to the grid, so surplus solar is simply lost. The tools must be forced to zero export or they invent arbitrage revenue.
  • The meter reading day. KEPCO billing months follow the customer's reading day, not the calendar month, which shifts which peaks fall in which bill.

Two dispatch runs, one decision

Every candidate size is run twice. The first run is a linear program with perfect knowledge of the year, which gives the theoretical maximum saving and is the same kind of answer that HOMER, REopt and DER-VET produce. The second run is a rule based controller that sees only the previous 14 days, sets its peak target with a 20 kW margin, charges at night, absorbs surplus solar and never exports. On the factory below the realistic run captured about 81% of the theoretical saving. The verdict on the cover of the report is based on the realistic run; the theoretical run is printed beside it so that a reader of a HOMER or REopt report knows which number to compare with.

How the cross checks came out

The factory is a one shift manufacturer with about 506 kWp of rooftop solar in the plan and a 628 kWh, 191 kW battery, analysed on a full year of actual 15 minute data. We ran four international tools on the same load, the same solar profile and the same battery, then rebilled every dispatch with our own tariff calculator so that the comparison isolates the dispatch and not the billing. Money is shown as ratios to protect the client.

The pattern is consistent. Tools that optimise with full knowledge land within a few percent of our optimal run once the dispatch is rebilled correctly, and tools with built in controllers written for other markets fall far short. None of them produce the realistic number by themselves. That is the number the owner will see on the bill, so that is the number we decide on.

Things that went wrong, so you do not repeat them

  • DER-VET with binary charge or discharge variables and the free solver stalled for eleven hours in May. The linear setting finished in 58 seconds with the same result.
  • The DER-VET template carries a 100 kW auxiliary load for the battery by default. Left in, it looks like a 100 kW export and turns the run into energy arbitrage.
  • Limiting the demand charge window to weekday business hours in DER-VET made it charge at 191 kW just before the window opened and raised the annual peak above the no battery case. The KEPCO ratchet applies to all hours, so the window must be all hours.
  • SAM's headline 'annual AC energy' is the energy delivered to the site including battery discharge and excluding curtailed solar, not the solar yield. We label it as such in the annex.
  • The REopt web tool takes 20 to 50 minutes per run and its results page can freeze a browser; the PDF download of the result is the reliable route.

What the finished study contains

  • A one page verdict: recommended, conditional or not recommended, decided by the realistic payback, with the three or four site conditions that could change it.
  • The data quality page: interval count equals days times 96, gaps and duplicates repaired and listed, billing months by reading day.
  • The load map, the monthly maximum demand with and without solar, and the dispatch on the worst day.
  • The capacity sweep from 250 to 2,000 kWh with saving, NPV, IRR and payback for each size; a size is called suitable only when it ranks first on NPV.
  • The 20 year cash flow as an editable spreadsheet whose formulas are live, so a lender can change the discount rate or the tariff escalation.
  • The cross check annex with the inputs given to each tool, the comparison table and the original output pages of each program.
  • An operation monitor page showing a representative weekday and holiday with the charge and discharge times, so the owner can see what the EMS will do.

The published case in one paragraph

The same factory, written up without names in our Korean articles, exceeded 600 kW for only 19 hours in a year, mostly in the morning before the roof produces. Solar alone cut the bill to an index of 68 from 100 and left the morning peak; solar plus battery reached 58. The simple paybacks were 5.7 years for solar only, 6.4 years for solar plus battery and 8.8 years for the battery on its own, on a 20 year horizon at 4.5% with no tariff increase. The same case also shows how sensitive the answer is: moving from the planning capacity to the as designed capacity, derating the PCS, adding 2% a year ageing and an inverter replacement lengthens the battery's own payback by several years. That is the point of the study: the assumptions decide, and they must be written down.
